Teaching a voice model to speak Somali

GLM-4-Voice is a 9-billion-parameter speech-to-speech chat model. Out of the box it answers Somali speech in Chinese or English. This page follows one utterance through all four of its models — animated, box by box — and covers what we froze, what we trained, the data we used, what went wrong, and every training round so far.

01

GLM-4-Voice is not one model. It is four.

Speech goes in, gets turned into tokens, a language model thinks in those tokens, and two more models turn the answer back into sound. Knowing which box does what told us what to train.

The key idea The audio tokens carry what is said, and mostly not who is saying it. The flow decoder adds the voice. So we could split the problem in two: teach the LLM Somali from many speakers (Track A), and teach the decoder one voice from Omar alone (Track B). The two tracks use separate data and separate models. They only meet at inference time.
02

Everything downstream is integers

The first box is the one we never touched, and the one the whole project depended on. If it can't represent Somali sounds, nothing further along can fix that.

The tokens

Already in the vocabulary

<|audio_0|> to <|audio_16383|> are ordinary tokens in the 9B's vocabulary. There was no vocab resize and no new embeddings to train — the model could already read and write them, it just had nothing Somali to say.

The rate

12.5 tokens per second

A 10-second clip is 125 tokens. That low rate is why a whole conversational turn fits inside a 1,024-token training sample alongside its text.

Step 0 · go / no-go

We tested it before training anything

The tokenizer was trained on Chinese and English, so it might have merged Somali sounds (c, x, q, dh). 50 Somali clips and 20 Omar clips went audio → tokens → stock decoder → audio, and we listened. The judge's error rose from 0.155 to 0.320 — worse, but unmistakably still Somali. Go.

03

What we froze, what we trained, and why

Each choice came from one question: where does the missing ability live? Somali is a language ability, so it lives in the LLM. A voice is an acoustic one, so it lives in the decoder.

Frozen

Speech tokenizer

  • Its tokens are already in the LLM's vocabulary, so there was no vocab resize and no new embeddings.
  • Retraining it would change what every token means, which would break both the 9B and the decoder at once.
LoRA · r=64, α=128

GLM-4-Voice 9B

  • This is where language lives, and it didn't know Somali.
  • A full fine-tune of 9B needs optimizer state for every weight, far more than one GPU holds. LoRA trains 169 M weights (1.7%) and fits in bf16 on one 96 GB card.
  • Adapters on all attention and MLP layers (query_key_value, dense, dense_h_to_4h, dense_4h_to_h). Most language knowledge sits in the MLPs.
  • Rank 64 instead of the usual 8–16, because a new language is a large change, not a style tweak.
  • bf16, not 4-bit QLoRA. We had the memory, and 4-bit costs quality.
Full fine-tune

Flow decoder

  • The voice is baked into the weights. The config has n_spks: 1 and inference always passes a zero speaker embedding, so there's no speaker slot to swap.
  • The flow model is small (~100 M params) and we had 61 h of one speaker, so we trained all of it.
  • The token embedding stays frozen for the first 6,000 steps. That keeps "which token means which sound" fixed while the voice adapts, so content doesn't drift.
Frozen

HiFT vocoder

  • Vocoders generalize across speakers well, and Omar's timbre came out without buzzy artifacts.
  • Retraining it needs HiFi-GAN discriminators that this repo doesn't include.
  • Constraint: our mel spectrograms must match CosyVoice's settings exactly, because this frozen vocoder only understands mels made that way.
Full fine-tune · judge

MMS-1b-all, Somali

  • We needed something to score Somali speech. Whisper can't do Somali, so it couldn't be the judge.
  • MMS already has a Somali adapter (CER 0.34 on our test), so it starts well above zero.
  • 960 M of 965 M weights trained. Only the CNN feature encoder is frozen: those low-level acoustic filters don't depend on language.
Blending instead of replacing At merge time --scale multiplies each LoRA module's α/r factor before folding it in: 1.0 is the full adapter, 0.3 is a third of the delta, verified numerically as 0.30× the weight-delta norm. It's a real knob, but it treats a symptom — if a fine-tune has to be dialled down to behave, the training data is usually the actual problem.
04

Speaking the model's own language

We didn't invent a format. Every training sample uses the exact chat format the demo uses at inference. If training and inference differ by even one token, the model behaves unpredictably. A selftest stage checks that our token IDs match what the demo's own tokenization produces, before any training data is built.

[gMASK]<sop><|system|>\n{system}<|user|>\n{audio or text}<|assistant|>{mode}\n{target}<|user|>
prompt: loss masked (−100) learned

The final <|user|> is how the model says "my turn is over". If it isn't learned, the model never stops talking. The loss only covers target positions, so we never compute fp32 logits over the 168,960-token vocabulary for the prompt. That saves a lot of memory.

The 13 : 26 rhythm

GLM-4-Voice streams: it writes a little text, then the audio for that text, then more text. It was pre-trained with 13 text tokens, then 26 audio tokens (about 2 seconds of speech), repeating. We kept that exact ratio, so we teach Somali without re-teaching how streaming works.

Why dialogue is gone Rounds 1 and 2 trained on it, and the objective was literally "given what one podcast guest just said, produce what the other guest said next". Podcast guests agree, interrupt, repeat the last phrase back and change the subject — which is exactly what the round-2 demo did. Round 3 drops the task entirely with build_sft.py --tasks asr,tts. ASR teaches the model to hear Somali and TTS teaches it to speak Somali; neither says anything about how to behave in a conversation, so the base model's own assistant manners are left alone.
05

Content and voice are trained apart

Because audio tokens carry what is said and almost nothing about who said it, the two halves of the problem could be trained on different data, in different models, and only meet at inference.

Track A · what is said

Many speakers, one language

Every usable clip from all five channels, from as many voices as possible. Speaker identity is noise here: ASR and TTS never look at the speaker label, so more voices only makes the Somali more robust.

Track B · how it sounds

One speaker, verified

8,163 clips that ECAPA speaker verification confirms are Omar (≥ 0.55 cosine per clip, ≥ 0.60 median per video). A guest's voice in the training set would blur the result, so two whole videos were dropped.

06

The data

We never copy audio around. Every clip in the pipeline is just a start and end time inside an episode's clean.flac (24 kHz, denoised with DeepFilterNet3, loudness-normalized), cut in memory when needed. The full processed tree is 157 GB and wouldn't fit on the 116 GB of free disk. Streaming slices and keeping only tokens and mels made it work.

Two transcriptions of the same windows

The transcripts are the text source of truth for everything, and the first ones were the project's biggest problem (more below).

Round 1 labels

MAI-Transcribe-2

Microsoft's model via OpenRouter. It doesn't support Somali. With auto language detection it often guessed Urdu, Estonian, Arabic or English, and some windows came back in Arabic or even Japanese script.

Rounds 2 and 3 labels

ElevenLabs Scribe v2, forced som

Real Somali on every clip we tested. 7,983 windows pass the Somali check (88.5 h), 1,066 came back empty (music-heavy), 892 fail. It cost about 140,000 credits, roughly $25 for 110 h, or 23 cents an audio hour.

07

Training rounds, in order

Each step had a reason to come before the next. We checked the audio stack first, built a judge, trained the voice (it was cheap), then trained the language — twice over, before the demo told us what was actually wrong.

Step 0 · go / no-go

Can the frozen audio stack even carry Somali?

A LoRA on the LLM can't fix a tokenizer that loses Somali sounds. So before any training, 50 random Somali clips and 20 Omar clips went through tokenizer → stock decoder and back, and we listened. It was intelligible. The judge's error went from 0.155 on the original audio to 0.320 on the resynthesized version: worse, but clearly still Somali. Go.

check_resynthesis.py50 + 20 clips
Round · MMS judge

Build a Somali ear

Full CTC fine-tune of MMS-1b-all on 62 h (our segments plus IbrahimDayax). 3 epochs, 7,152 steps, about 65 minutes. We kept the best-CER checkpoint.

lr 3e-5batch 16×2CER 0.341 → 0.208ext test 0.100 → 0.056
Track B · flow decoder

Give it Omar's voice

30,000 steps at a low learning rate: adapting a voice, not learning from scratch. Batches are sized by mel frames (24,000, about 280 s of audio) instead of clip count, which keeps memory constant as clip lengths vary. Resynthesized Somali became more intelligible in Omar's voice than in the stock voice.

Replace the transcripts, not the model

After finding how much of the MAI text wasn't Somali, we tested alternatives on its worst clips and relabeled all 9,941 windows with Scribe v2, forcing Somali. Same windows and same file schema, so the pipeline could read either set unchanged.

10

What went wrong, and what we learned

The bugs were the most useful part of this project, so they're all listed here.

1

We labeled a Somali corpus with a model that doesn't speak Somali

The whole first round of transcripts came from MAI-Transcribe-2 with auto language detection. Many windows were real Somali, many weren't: Urdu, Estonian, Arabic script, even Japanese. Round-1 LoRA and MMS both trained on this text, and it was also the test reference.

Fix: tested alternatives on MAI's worst clips. Meta's Muse ignored language=so and replied in Arabic script. Scribe v2 with auto detection still misfired (English, Arabic, Portuguese, Finnish). Scribe v2 with Somali forced returned Somali on every clip, so we relabeled everything with it.
2

We taught it to continue a conversation, then wondered why it wouldn't answer one

Round 2 spoke fluent Somali with correct captions, echoed the user and answered beside the point. The dialogue objective was "given what one guest said, produce what the other said next" — 22–35 k pairs of it, rank-64 adapters on every projection, two rounds deep. Podcast guests agree, interrupt and change the subject. It learned exactly what we taught it.

Fix: round 3 drops the task (--tasks asr,tts, --no-pairs) and starts from the stock 9B, because rounds 1 and 2 both carry the habit. ASR and TTS teach hearing and speaking; conversational manners are left to the instruction-tuned base.
3

A "clean data" filter threw away two thirds of the corpus

Round 3 was first built with --max-speakers 1, on the assumption that two-speaker windows meant overlapping voices. Measuring it showed 0.0 hours of overlap in the entire corpus: Scribe never emits overlapping segments, and every clip is one voice either way. The filter cost 78.1 h of perfectly good audio.

Lesson: measure the property you think you're filtering on before you filter on it.
4

Re-transcribing single speakers didn't help, because it was the same model

We cut 44,241 one-speaker clips and sent each to MAI on its own, hoping shorter and cleaner input would help. It was still guessing languages. 27,304 were kept only after an agreement filter: drop a clip when the new text and the old window text disagree by more than 15% CER, because two passes disagreeing usually means a wrong-language guess.

5

Our test scores were graded against noisy answers

MMS "0.208 CER on our test" is measured against MAI text, so part of that error is the label's fault, not the model's. The IbrahimDayax test set, with clean human text, is the more trustworthy number, and it improved just as much (−44%).

Next: retrain on Scribe labels and score against Scribe test labels.
6

Two demo bugs that no loss curve could show

Greedy decoding: model_server.py called generate() without do_sample=True, so the temperature and top-p it was handed were silently ignored. Every reply was the argmax path, and a model fine-tuned on short turns loops that way ("Waa yahay. Waa yahay. …"). The wrong system prompt: orb_demo.py's built-in prompt is English, so the Somali model answered in English.

Fix: pass do_sample and a repetition_penalty (default 1.1, GLM_REPETITION_PENALTY to override); GLM_LANG=so selects a Somali prompt and GLM_SYSTEM_PROMPT overrides it. Listen to the model before drawing conclusions about the data.
7

"Omar's" clips weren't all Omar

Among the videos were a clip of the Namibian president, another host, and an Arabic poetry recitation. Training the voice on those would have blurred it.

Fix: ECAPA speaker verification against Omar's median voice print, per clip and per video.
8

The disk kept running out

157 GB of processed audio vs 116 GB free, and MMS checkpoints at 11 GB each.

Fix: never copy audio (cut from clean.flac in memory), download only the clean.flac of transcribed episodes, delete MMS resume checkpoints once final/ exists, check free space before training, and back up runs to a private bucket every 10 minutes.
9

The small stuff that cost hours

A bucket sync that stalled while matching 300k paths (fixed with 16 parallel direct copies). Audio prep running at 27 clips/s (fixed with raw int16 bytes and GPU resampling: about 100× faster Arrow writes, at half the size). datasets 2.18 refusing to save empty splits. Trainer resume breaking on torch ≥ 2.6 until numpy types were allowlisted in rng_state.pth. Audio token IDs that had to stay inside the tokenizer's string range.

11

What we swapped out

Components we replaced along the way, and why.

MAI-Transcribe-2 (auto language)→ElevenLabs Scribe v2, som forcedThe labeler has to actually support the language. See above.
Whisper as the intelligibility judge→Fine-tuned MMS-1b-all (Somali)Whisper can't transcribe Somali. MMS has a Somali head, and fine-tuning it made it a much better judge.
Dialogue-pair training→ASR + TTS onlyConversation continuation isn't question answering. The base model already knows how to be an assistant; it only needed the language.
Stock flow decoder voice→Omar flow decoder (drop-in flow.pt)Same architecture and file. Only the weights change, so inference code is untouched.
torch 2.3 (upstream pins)→torch / torchaudio 2.9.1 + cu128The RTX PRO 6000 is Blackwell (sm_120). torch 2.3 has no kernels for it.
QLoRA (4-bit base)→bf16 LoRAWith 96 GB there was no need to quantize. bf16 has better quality and no dequantization overhead. QLoRA stays as a fallback for small cards.
torchaudio file I/O→soundfiletorchaudio 2.9 needs torchcodec to read audio. A shim also keeps speechbrain (ECAPA) working.
torchvision, deepspeed in the training image→removedtorchvision had an ABI mismatch with torch 2.9. deepspeed broke accelerate without CUDA_HOME. Neither was needed.
Demo decoder: 10 Euler steps × 2 guidance passes→6 steps, guidance batchedFor the live voice-orb demo: 333 → 108 ms per chunk, with the mel within ~1% of the 10-step output.
